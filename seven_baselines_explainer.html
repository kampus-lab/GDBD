七条底线 The Seven Baselines

In August 2013, China's internet industry gathered in Beijing and agreed to a set of seven rules for "acceptable" online conduct — officially framed as a clean-up of online rumors. Read in the order Beijing published them, the list looks unremarkable. Read for what actually gets enforced, it reveals what the state was really protecting.

02 — Why it was framed as an anti-rumor drive

The stated problem, and the actual one

The official narrative was about protecting the public from harmful misinformation. The enforcement record tells a narrower story.

The public case

The campaign launched alongside a Public Security Bureau crackdown on people accused of fabricating and spreading online rumors for profit — extortion, stock manipulation, manufactured outrage. State media held these arrests up as proof the internet needed cleaning up.

Cases cited at the time: Qin Huohuo · Li'er Chaisi · Zhou Lubao · Fu Xuesheng

The structural tell

Rule seven — information accuracy — sits dead last. Rules two and three, protecting the "socialist system" and vaguely defined "national interests," sit near the top. A policy genuinely aimed at truthful information would not bury accuracy at the bottom of its own list.

Commentary from the Communist Party's Guangdong paper at the time cast the baselines as ordinary civic responsibility — every citizen's duty to avoid spreading and believing rumors.

04 — Where this connects

Part of a larger pattern

The Seven Baselines were a domestic speech-control measure, aimed at Chinese internet users inside China. But the same instinct — treating "national interest" and "socialist system" conformity as non-negotiable, and information accuracy as secondary — shows up again a few years later, once ByteDance takes a Chinese-built recommendation engine and a Chinese content-moderation playbook global. Leaked TikTok moderation guidance from 2019 shows the same hierarchy at work abroad: bans on content about Tiananmen Square, Tibetan independence, and Falun Gong, enforced by Beijing-based moderators even for a platform marketed as a neutral global product.
